## Starting a Business in Utah

One of the first decisions a business owner must make is how to legally structure the company.

Choosing an entity affects personal liability, ownership rights, management responsibilities, taxation, and what happens when an owner leaves or dies.

### LLCs, Corporations, and Partnerships

Utah recognizes several business structures, each with different legal requirements and advantages.

**Limited Liability Company (LLC)**

An LLC is a popular structure for small businesses because it generally separates the company's legal obligations from the personal liabilities of its owners.

LLCs also offer flexibility in how the company is managed and how owners share profits and responsibilities.

However, forming an LLC does not automatically protect owners from every type of personal liability. Personal guarantees, individual misconduct, and failure to maintain the separation between personal and business affairs can create additional risks.

**Corporation**

Corporations may be appropriate for companies seeking outside investment, issuing stock, or establishing a more formal ownership and management structure.

Corporations generally operate through shareholders, directors, and officers, with responsibilities governed by applicable law and corporate documents.

**Partnership**

Partnerships allow two or more parties to operate a business together. Different partnership structures provide different levels of management flexibility and liability protection.

A general partnership may expose partners to personal liability for business obligations, while certain registered partnership structures offer additional protections.

The appropriate choice depends on the business's goals, ownership arrangements, financing needs, and tax considerations.

Utah provides an overview of these options through its [business structure guide](https://www.utah.gov/business/starting/structure.html).

### Registering Your Business

Forming a business in Utah typically involves selecting an available name, choosing the appropriate entity type, filing formation documents, and designating a registered agent when required.

Depending on the business, additional registrations, permits, or licenses may be necessary.

The [Utah Division of Corporations and Commercial Code](https://commerce.utah.gov/corporations/) manages the state's business entity registration system.

You can also use the state's [Business Registration System](https://businessregistration.utah.gov/) to access registration services.

Registration is an important first step, but it is only part of establishing a business's legal foundation. Internal agreements and clear ownership arrangements can be just as important as the documents filed with the state.

## Operating Agreements, Bylaws, and Ownership Agreements

Many business disputes begin with questions that the owners never clearly answered.

Who gets to make important decisions? How are profits distributed? What happens if an owner wants to leave? Can one partner sell their ownership interest without the others agreeing?

These questions are easier to address when expectations are documented before a disagreement occurs.

### LLC Operating Agreements

An operating agreement establishes rules for how an LLC is owned and managed.

Depending on the company, it may address:

- Ownership percentages and capital contributions
- Voting rights and management responsibilities
- How profits and losses are allocated
- Procedures for admitting new members
- Restrictions on transferring ownership interests
- What happens when a member dies, becomes disabled, or leaves
- Procedures for resolving disagreements or dissolving the company

Even single-member LLCs can benefit from clearly documenting how the company operates.

### Corporate Bylaws and Shareholder Agreements

Corporations often use bylaws and shareholder agreements to establish their internal governance and ownership arrangements.

These documents may define how directors are selected, how major decisions are approved, and what rights shareholders have when ownership changes.

### Partnership and Buy-Sell Agreements

For businesses with multiple owners, a buy-sell agreement can establish what happens when someone retires, dies, becomes disabled, or wants to sell their interest.

The agreement may specify how ownership is valued, who has the right to purchase an interest, and how the purchase will be funded.

**Clear agreements help business owners make decisions with confidence and reduce the likelihood of expensive disputes.**

## Business Contracts and Commercial Agreements

Contracts are part of nearly every business relationship. They determine what each party must do, when payment is required, how responsibilities are divided, and what happens if something goes wrong.

A contract that appears straightforward can contain terms that create significant legal or financial exposure.

Our business law services include drafting, reviewing, and negotiating agreements such as:

- Vendor and supplier contracts
- Independent contractor and consulting agreements
- Customer and service agreements
- Confidentiality and nondisclosure agreements
- Partnership and ownership agreements
- Commercial purchase and sale agreements
- Business financing and related transaction documents

### What Should a Business Contract Include?

Although every agreement is different, a well-drafted business contract should clearly define the relationship between the parties.

Important provisions often include:

**Scope of work:** What products, services, or responsibilities must each party provide?

**Payment terms:** How much is owed, when is payment due, and what happens if a payment is late?

**Termination:** Under what circumstances can either party end the agreement?

**Liability and indemnification:** Who is responsible if a loss, claim, or legal dispute arises?

**Dispute resolution:** How will disagreements be handled, and what law or forum will govern the dispute?

**Confidentiality and intellectual property:** Who owns work created during the relationship, and what information must remain confidential?

These provisions can be especially important when a business signs a long-term agreement, enters a new partnership, or takes on a substantial financial obligation.

Having an attorney review a significant contract before signing can help identify risks that may otherwise be overlooked.

## Buying or Selling a Business in Utah

Buying or selling a company is often one of the largest financial transactions a business owner will undertake.

Beyond the purchase price, the transaction may involve existing contracts, outstanding debts, employee obligations, intellectual property, real estate, and potential liabilities.

A carefully structured transaction can help both parties understand exactly what is being purchased and what obligations will remain.

### Buying a Business

Before purchasing a company, it is important to understand what you are acquiring.

Legal due diligence may involve examining:

- Ownership and organizational documents
- Existing contracts and financial obligations
- Pending claims or litigation
- Commercial leases and real estate interests
- Intellectual property rights
- Employee and contractor arrangements
- Required licenses and regulatory obligations

Buyers should also understand the difference between an asset purchase and an equity purchase.

In an **asset purchase**, the buyer acquires specified business assets and assumes agreed obligations, subject to applicable law.

In an **equity purchase**, the buyer acquires ownership interests in the company itself, which may carry existing liabilities and obligations.

The appropriate structure depends on the business, financial arrangements, tax considerations, and risks involved.

### Selling a Business

Selling a business requires more than finding a buyer and agreeing on a price.

The transaction may require negotiating payment terms, providing financial and legal disclosures, transferring ownership interests, addressing outstanding obligations, and establishing conditions for closing.

Business owners may also need to consider their financial and estate plans before finalizing a sale.

## Business Succession Planning

For many business owners, the company represents years or decades of work. It may also be one of the family's most valuable financial assets.

Yet many businesses operate without a clear plan for what happens when an owner retires, becomes incapacitated, or dies.

Without a succession plan, the company may face uncertainty about who can make decisions, who owns the business, and whether operations can continue.

### Preparing for an Ownership Transition

A business succession plan may address:

- Who will manage the company when an owner steps away
- Whether ownership will transfer to family members, employees, or outside buyers
- How business interests will be valued
- How ownership transfers will be funded
- What happens if an owner dies or becomes incapacitated
- How to minimize disruption to employees, customers, and business operations

Succession planning is particularly important for family-owned companies, where business decisions and family relationships often overlap.

A transition may also require coordination with an owner's personal estate plan.

**A good succession plan does more than explain who inherits the business. It helps establish how the company can continue operating when leadership changes.**

## Protecting Business Assets and Managing Legal Risk

Business owners regularly make decisions that expose their companies to financial and legal risk.

Some risks arise from contracts or business relationships. Others involve ownership structure, personal guarantees, regulatory requirements, or the way business and personal assets are managed.

An effective legal foundation can help reduce unnecessary exposure.

Depending on the circumstances, asset protection planning may involve:

- Selecting and maintaining an appropriate business entity
- Keeping personal and business finances separate
- Reviewing personal guarantees and contractual obligations
- Defining ownership and management responsibilities
- Evaluating liability provisions in significant agreements
- Coordinating business ownership with estate planning

No entity structure or legal document eliminates every possible business risk.

However, identifying potential problems early can provide more options than addressing them after a claim or dispute arises.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Business Law in Utah

### Do I need an attorney to start an LLC in Utah?

Utah generally allows individuals to register an LLC without hiring an attorney.

However, registration alone does not resolve questions about ownership, liability, management, taxes, or what happens when an owner leaves.

An attorney can help you choose an appropriate structure and prepare agreements that reflect how the company will actually operate.

### Does forming an LLC protect my personal assets?

An LLC generally provides a level of separation between the company's obligations and its owners' personal assets.

However, that protection has limits. Personal guarantees, certain wrongful acts, and other circumstances may expose an owner to personal liability.

Maintaining proper records and keeping personal and business finances separate can also be important.

### Does a single-member LLC need an operating agreement?

Utah law does not generally require a single-member LLC to adopt a written operating agreement as a condition of formation.

Nevertheless, a written agreement can document management arrangements, ownership rights, and procedures for important decisions.

It can also be useful when dealing with lenders, future investors, or ownership transitions.

### What happens if my business partner wants to leave?

The answer depends on the company's governing documents, ownership structure, and applicable law.

An operating agreement, shareholder agreement, or buy-sell agreement may define how an owner can withdraw and how their interest will be valued or purchased.

Without an agreement, resolving the transition may be more complicated.

### Can I sell my business without selling the entire company?

Possibly. A business transaction can involve a sale of selected assets, a partial ownership interest, or the entire company.

Each structure carries different legal, financial, and tax considerations.

An attorney can help review the proposed transaction and prepare the necessary agreements.

### When should I create a business succession plan?

Ideally, succession planning begins before a transition becomes urgent.

Business owners should consider creating or updating a plan when establishing a company, adding partners, preparing for retirement, or making significant changes in ownership.

Waiting until an owner becomes incapacitated or dies can leave important decisions unresolved.

### What should I do before signing a major business contract?

Read the entire agreement and understand its payment, termination, liability, and dispute resolution provisions.

Pay particular attention to personal guarantees, automatic renewals, ownership of intellectual property, and obligations that continue after the contract ends.

A legal review can help identify terms that may not be obvious from the agreement's general description.

### Can a business attorney help prevent disputes?

An attorney cannot guarantee that disputes will never occur.

However, clear contracts, appropriate entity structures, documented ownership arrangements, and proactive succession planning can reduce uncertainty and help businesses manage disagreements more effectively.
