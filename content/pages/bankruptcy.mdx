## Understanding Bankruptcy in Utah

Bankruptcy is a legal process governed by federal law that allows qualifying individuals and businesses to seek relief from debts they cannot afford to repay.

For individuals, the two most common types are **Chapter 7** and **Chapter 13** bankruptcy. Both can provide relief from eligible debts, but they work differently.

The right choice depends on several factors, including your income, the type and amount of debt you owe, and the property you want to protect.

### Chapter 7 Bankruptcy: Eliminating Eligible Debt

Chapter 7 is sometimes called *liquidation bankruptcy*. It allows qualifying individuals to discharge many unsecured debts without entering a long-term repayment plan.

Common examples of debts that may be discharged include:

- Credit card balances
- Medical bills
- Personal loans
- Certain unpaid utility bills
- Other qualifying unsecured debts

In a Chapter 7 case, a court-appointed trustee reviews your finances and determines whether you own any nonexempt property that can be sold to pay creditors. Many individuals who file Chapter 7 have no nonexempt assets available for liquidation.

**You do not necessarily lose your home, car, or personal belongings when you file Chapter 7.** Utah law provides exemptions that protect certain property, subject to applicable limits and other legal requirements.

Chapter 7 is generally completed within a few months, although complicated cases can take longer. Eligibility depends partly on your income and financial circumstances, including whether you satisfy the applicable bankruptcy means test.

Learn more from the [United States Courts' Chapter 7 Bankruptcy Guide](https://www.uscourts.gov/court-programs/bankruptcy/bankruptcy-basics/chapter-7-bankruptcy-basics).

### Chapter 13 Bankruptcy: Repaying Debt Over Time

Chapter 13 bankruptcy allows eligible individuals with regular income to reorganize their debts through a court-approved repayment plan.

Instead of immediately eliminating eligible debts, you make scheduled payments over a period that generally lasts **three to five years**. Depending on your financial situation, some unsecured debts may be discharged after you successfully complete the plan.

Chapter 13 may be worth considering if you:

- Have regular income but cannot keep up with existing debt payments
- Have fallen behind on mortgage payments and want to prevent foreclosure
- Want to keep property that might be at risk in Chapter 7
- Need time to catch up on certain secured or priority debts
- Do not qualify for Chapter 7 relief

Chapter 13 can provide a structured way to address outstanding debt while potentially allowing you to keep important assets.

However, the repayment plan must meet legal requirements, and you must be able to make the required payments.

Learn more from the [United States Courts' Chapter 13 Bankruptcy Guide](https://www.uscourts.gov/court-programs/bankruptcy/bankruptcy-basics/chapter-13-bankruptcy-basics).

## Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 13: Which Is Right for You?

Although both forms of bankruptcy can offer debt relief, their requirements and consequences are different.

| | Chapter 7 | Chapter 13 |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary purpose** | Discharge eligible debts | Reorganize and repay debts |
| **Typical timeline** | A few months | Three to five years |
| **Income requirements** | May require passing a means test | Generally requires regular income |
| **Repayment plan** | No long-term repayment plan | Court-approved repayment plan |
| **Property** | Nonexempt assets may be sold | Debtors generally retain property while making required payments |
| **Foreclosure** | May temporarily delay foreclosure | May allow qualifying debtors to catch up on missed mortgage payments |

These distinctions are only a starting point. The best option depends on your full financial picture, including your income, assets, debts, and long-term goals.

## Can Bankruptcy Stop Creditors From Contacting You?

One of the most immediate protections available through bankruptcy is the **automatic stay**.

In most cases, filing a bankruptcy petition triggers a federal court order that temporarily stops many creditor collection activities.

While the automatic stay is in effect, it may prevent creditors from:

- Continuing collection calls and demands for payment
- Starting or continuing certain lawsuits
- Garnishing your wages
- Pursuing certain bank account levies
- Proceeding with foreclosure or repossession efforts

The automatic stay has important exceptions and limitations. Some proceedings can continue, and creditors may ask the bankruptcy court for permission to resume collection activities. Additional restrictions may apply if you have filed for bankruptcy previously.

If you are facing a pending foreclosure, wage garnishment, repossession, or collection lawsuit, the timing of a bankruptcy filing can be especially important.

**Speak with an attorney before assuming that bankruptcy will stop a specific collection action.**

## What Happens to Your Home, Car, and Other Property?

A common concern about bankruptcy is whether you will lose everything you own.

Fortunately, bankruptcy law recognizes that people need certain assets to maintain their households and continue working.

Utah has laws that protect qualifying property from creditors. These protections are called *exemptions*.

Depending on your circumstances, exemptions may apply to certain interests in:

- Your primary residence
- A motor vehicle
- Household furnishings and appliances
- Clothing and personal belongings
- Retirement accounts and certain benefits
- Tools or equipment used for work

Exemptions have legal limits and eligibility requirements. Property that is protected in one situation may not be protected in another.

Additionally, a bankruptcy exemption does not automatically eliminate a lender's mortgage or security interest in property.

Utah generally requires qualifying residents to use applicable state exemptions rather than federal bankruptcy exemptions. Your residence history may affect which exemption laws apply.

You can review the [Utah Bankruptcy Court's Chapter 7 filing resources](https://www.utb.uscourts.gov/chapter-7-individual-debtor-bankruptcy-petition-package) for more information about property schedules and exemptions.

**Before filing, it is important to understand exactly which assets may be protected and which could be at risk.**

## What Debts Can Bankruptcy Eliminate?

Bankruptcy can discharge many types of unsecured debt, but it does not eliminate every financial obligation.

### Debts that may be discharged

Depending on your circumstances and the type of bankruptcy you file, dischargeable debts commonly include:

- Credit card debt
- Medical debt
- Qualifying personal loans
- Certain old utility balances
- Some civil judgments
- Other eligible unsecured obligations

### Debts that are generally not discharged

Certain debts receive special treatment under federal bankruptcy law. Examples include:

- Child support and alimony
- Most student loans, unless the debtor establishes grounds for discharge under applicable law
- Certain tax debts
- Criminal fines and restitution
- Debts arising from particular types of fraud or misconduct, when the legal requirements for nondischargeability are met

Secured debts, such as mortgages and auto loans, require additional consideration because a bankruptcy discharge does not automatically remove the creditor's lien against the property.

An attorney can review your specific obligations and explain which debts may be discharged, repaid, or remain after bankruptcy.

## What Is the Bankruptcy Process?

The process varies depending on whether you file Chapter 7 or Chapter 13, but most individual bankruptcy cases involve several common steps.

### 1. Review Your Financial Situation

The first step is to understand your income, expenses, outstanding debts, and assets.

This review helps determine whether bankruptcy is appropriate and which type of filing may be available.

### 2. Complete Required Credit Counseling

With limited exceptions, individuals must complete an approved credit counseling course within 180 days before filing for bankruptcy.

This requirement applies even if you already know you want to file.

### 3. Prepare and File Your Bankruptcy Petition

A bankruptcy case begins when a petition and supporting documents are filed with the bankruptcy court.

These documents disclose information about your finances, including income, expenses, property, debts, and recent financial transactions.

Most Utah bankruptcy cases are filed in the **United States Bankruptcy Court for the District of Utah**.

### 4. Attend the Meeting of Creditors

After filing, you generally attend a meeting of creditors, commonly called a *341 meeting*.

A bankruptcy trustee reviews your case and asks questions about your financial disclosures. Creditors may also attend and ask relevant questions.

Despite its name, this meeting is not typically a courtroom trial.

### 5. Complete the Remaining Requirements

In Chapter 7, the trustee administers the case, and eligible individuals may receive a discharge after satisfying the required conditions.

In Chapter 13, you must generally complete the payments required under your court-approved plan before receiving a discharge.

Individual debtors are also generally required to complete an approved financial management course before discharge.

For additional information, visit the [United States Courts' Bankruptcy Basics](https://www.uscourts.gov/court-programs/bankruptcy/bankruptcy-basics/process-bankruptcy-basics).

## Are There Alternatives to Bankruptcy?

Not every financial problem requires a bankruptcy filing.

Depending on your income, debts, and available resources, other approaches may be worth considering.

**Debt settlement:** You may be able to negotiate with creditors to resolve certain debts for less than the full balance. Settlement can have tax, credit, and legal consequences.

**Payment arrangements:** Some creditors may agree to modify payment schedules, reduce interest rates, or offer hardship programs.

**Debt management plans:** A reputable credit counseling organization may help you arrange a structured repayment plan with participating creditors.

**Negotiating specific obligations:** If only one or two debts are creating the problem, addressing those obligations individually may be more practical than filing bankruptcy.

Each option has potential benefits and drawbacks. A careful review of your financial circumstances can help you avoid unnecessary legal costs and understand the risks before making a decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Bankruptcy in Utah

### Will filing for bankruptcy ruin my credit forever?

No. Bankruptcy can significantly affect your credit, but it does not remain on your credit report forever.

A Chapter 7 bankruptcy can generally remain on your credit report for up to 10 years, while a Chapter 13 bankruptcy is generally removed after seven years under the major credit bureaus' reporting practices.

The impact on your credit score depends on your individual credit history. It is possible to begin rebuilding credit after bankruptcy.

### Can I keep my house if I file for bankruptcy?

Possibly. Whether you can keep your home depends on factors such as the equity in the property, applicable exemptions, mortgage payment status, and the type of bankruptcy you file.

Chapter 13 may allow qualifying homeowners to catch up on overdue mortgage payments through a repayment plan, provided they meet the necessary requirements.

### Do I have to be unemployed to file for bankruptcy?

No. Many people who file for bankruptcy are employed.

Your income affects which bankruptcy options are available, but having a job does not automatically disqualify you from filing.

### Will my spouse have to file bankruptcy with me?

Not necessarily. Married individuals may file individually or jointly.

However, your spouse's income, jointly owned assets, and shared debts may affect your case even if only one spouse files.

### Can bankruptcy stop wage garnishment?

In many cases, filing for bankruptcy triggers an automatic stay that stops qualifying wage garnishment activity.

Certain obligations, including some domestic support payments, are treated differently. The timing of the filing and any previous bankruptcies may also affect the available protection.

### How much does it cost to file bankruptcy in Utah?

The total cost depends on the type of bankruptcy, court filing fees, attorney fees, and the complexity of your case.

The bankruptcy court publishes its official filing fees, which are separate from any fees charged by an attorney.

You can find additional filing information on the [District of Utah Bankruptcy Court website](https://www.utb.uscourts.gov/).

### How do I know whether I qualify for Chapter 7?

Chapter 7 eligibility depends on several factors, including the nature of your debts, your household income, allowable expenses, and any previous bankruptcy filings.

For many individuals with primarily consumer debts, an income-based means test helps determine whether a Chapter 7 filing may be considered abusive.

Earning more than Utah's median income does not automatically mean you cannot file Chapter 7. Additional calculations and exceptions may apply.
