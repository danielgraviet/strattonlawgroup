A Pleasant Grove man has a stroke on a Tuesday morning. His wife calls the bank to cover a mortgage payment and is told, politely, that she's not authorized on the account. She calls his doctor's office for an update and is told the same thing about his medical records. Nothing is wrong with their marriage or their finances. No one ever signed the one-page document that would have let her act.

This is the gap a **financial power of attorney** and an **advance health care directive** are built to close, and in Utah, both documents just got a significant update. Here's what each one does, what changed on January 1, 2026, and how they fit into the rest of your plan alongside a [will or trust](/will-vs-trust-utah).

## The Short Answer

- **A financial power of attorney** lets someone you trust (your "agent") manage your money, property, and business affairs if you can't. It's governed by Utah's Uniform Power of Attorney Act, recently renumbered to [Utah Code Title 75A, Chapter 2](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S104.html).
- **An advance health care directive** lets your agent make medical decisions for you and can spell out your wishes. As of **January 1, 2026**, it's governed by a brand-new law: the Uniform Health Care Decisions Act, at [Utah Code Title 75A, Chapter 9](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S101.html).
- **Most people need both**, in addition to a will or trust. One covers your finances; the other covers your body.

## What a Financial Power of Attorney Does

A power of attorney (POA) is a document where you (the "principal") give someone else (your "agent") legal authority to act for you. In Utah, it's governed by the Uniform Power of Attorney Act. The act used to live in Title 75, Chapter 9; the legislature renumbered it into [Title 75A, Chapter 2](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S104.html) as part of a broader 2024–2025 reorganization of Utah's probate and fiduciary code. **The substance didn't change, only the citation**, but if you're reading an older form, a template site, or an old blog post that cites "75-9," you're looking at the previous numbering.

A properly drafted POA can authorize your agent to:

- Manage bank accounts and pay bills
- Buy, sell, or manage real estate (see our [real estate services](/real-estate))
- Run or make decisions for a business you own (see our [business law services](/business))
- Handle insurance, taxes, and government benefits
- Manage retirement accounts and investments

A few things worth knowing under current law:

- **It's durable by default.** Under [Utah Code § 75A-2-104](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S104.html), a power of attorney stays in effect even if you later become incapacitated, unless the document says otherwise. That's the entire point: it's there for the moment you can't act for yourself.
- **It avoids a conservatorship.** Without a POA, the only way for someone to manage your finances after you lose capacity is for a court to appoint a conservator, a public, often slow process. A funded POA generally avoids that step entirely.
- **Utah has an optional statutory form.** [Utah Code § 75A-2-301](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S301.html) provides a sample form agents and institutions recognize, though a custom document can grant broader or narrower authority depending on your situation.
- **Your agent has real duties.** Under [Utah Code § 75A-2-114](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S114.html), an agent must act in good faith, within the authority granted, and in your best interest, not their own.

## What an Advance Health Care Directive Does (New for 2026)

This is the document that names a **health care agent** to make medical decisions when you can't make or communicate them yourself, and lets you record your own wishes in advance.

On **January 1, 2026**, Utah repealed its old Advance Health Care Directive Act (formerly Title 75, Chapter 2a) and replaced it with the **Uniform Health Care Decisions Act**, now at [Utah Code Title 75A, Chapter 9](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S101.html). It's a meaningful rewrite, not a renumbering, so documents and advice built on the old chapter are now out of date in a few specific ways.

### What the new law lets you do

- **Appoint a health care agent.** Under [Utah Code § 75A-9-107](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S107.html), you name someone to make medical decisions for you once a qualifying provider determines you've lost the capacity to make them yourself. Signing now requires only **one adult witness** (who can't be your agent, your agent's spouse, or staff at a facility where you live), and that witness can appear in person or by real-time video.
- **Leave health care instructions.** Under [Utah Code § 75A-9-106](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S106.html), you can record your wishes about treatment, life support, and end-of-life care in advance, so your family and doctors aren't guessing.
- **Add a stand-alone mental health care directive.** This is genuinely new. Under [Utah Code § 75A-9-108](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S108.html), you can now separately address psychiatric treatment, preferred facilities, medications, and crisis intervention, and even name a different agent just for mental health decisions than the one who handles your other medical care.

## If You Don't Have a Directive: Utah's New "Default Surrogate" List

This is one of the most useful things to understand about the new law, whether or not you ever get around to signing a directive. If you lose capacity and haven't named an agent, [Utah Code § 75A-9-111](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S111.html) now spells out exactly who is authorized to decide for you, in this order:

1. An adult you've informally identified to make the decision
2. Your spouse (with exceptions if divorce or legal separation is already underway)
3. Your adult child or parent
4. Your cohabitant
5. Your adult sibling
6. Your adult grandchild or grandparent
7. An adult who has routinely helped you with decisions over the prior six months
8. A stepchild you actively parented
9. An adult who has shown special care and concern and knows your values
10. In limited institutional settings, with no one else reasonably available, a physician

This "default surrogate" can act **without going to court**. It's a safety net, but it's also exactly that: a default. It may hand decision-making to a family member you wouldn't have chosen, in an order you don't control. Naming your own agent is the only way to make sure the right person, and only that person, has the authority.

## Power of Attorney vs. Advance Health Care Directive: Side by Side

| | Financial Power of Attorney | Advance Health Care Directive |
|---|---|---|
| **Covers** | Money, property, business, benefits | Medical decisions, treatment wishes, mental health care |
| **Governing law** | [Utah Code Title 75A, Chapter 2](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter2/75A-2-S104.html) | [Utah Code Title 75A, Chapter 9](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75A/Chapter9/75A-9-S101.html) (new 1/1/2026) |
| **Takes effect** | Immediately, unless written to "spring" on incapacity | When a qualifying provider finds you lack capacity |
| **Witnesses required** | Signed and acknowledged per the statutory requirements | One adult witness (in person or live video) |
| **If you have none** | A court must appoint a conservator | A statutory "default surrogate" list applies |
| **Avoids court involvement** | Yes, if signed before you need it | Yes, if signed before you need it |

## If You Already Have These Documents

If your power of attorney or health care directive was signed before 2026, it's likely still valid; Utah generally honors documents that complied with the law in effect when you signed them. But because the agent-appointment rules, the witnessing requirements, and the entire structure of the health care directive changed, this is a good moment for a quick review, especially if:

- Your directive doesn't address mental health care and you'd want it to
- You're not sure who your documents name, or whether that person is still the right choice
- Your POA still cites "Title 75, Chapter 9" and hasn't been looked at in several years
- You've moved to Utah from another state and brought documents with you

## Every Complete Plan Has Both, Plus a Will or Trust

A financial power of attorney and an advance health care directive handle the "what if I can't act for myself" problem while you're alive. They don't replace a will or [revocable living trust](/will-vs-trust-utah), which control what happens to your property after you die, or name a guardian for minor children. A complete Utah estate plan generally includes:

- A will or funded trust
- A financial power of attorney
- An advance health care directive, with a mental health directive if it applies to your family
- Updated beneficiary designations

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a power of attorney and an advance health care directive?

A financial power of attorney lets your agent manage money and property. An advance health care directive lets your agent make medical decisions and record your treatment wishes, and can now include a separate mental health care directive. Most people need both.

### What changed in Utah's advance health care directive law in 2026?

Utah repealed the old Advance Health Care Directive Act and replaced it, effective January 1, 2026, with the Uniform Health Care Decisions Act at Title 75A, Chapter 9. It adds a stand-alone mental health care directive, a default-surrogate priority list, and updated signing rules.

### Is a document I signed before 2026 still valid?

In most cases, yes, but it's worth a review since the forms, witnessing rules, and default-surrogate list all changed.

### Who decides for me if I have neither document and can't speak for myself?

Utah law appoints a "default surrogate" in a set order of priority under Utah Code § 75A-9-111, generally starting with someone you've informally identified, then your spouse, adult child or parent, and other close family.
