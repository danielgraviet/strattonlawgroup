You and your spouse finally sit down at the kitchen table to "get the will done." Ten minutes in, someone asks the question that stops everything: *Should we just have a will, or do we need a trust?*

It's the most common question Utah families ask when they start estate planning, and the internet makes it harder than it needs to be. Some sites say everyone needs a trust. Others say a will is all anyone needs. The truth is that it depends on what you own, who you're providing for, and how much you want to spare your family from court.

Here's how each one works under Utah law, and a simple way to figure out which fits your family.

## The Short Answer

- **A will** says who gets your property and who raises your minor children. It only takes effect after you die, and it **generally goes through probate court**.
- **A revocable living trust** holds your property during your life and passes it to your family afterward **without probate**, as long as your property is actually moved into the trust.
- **Many Utah families end up with both:** a trust that holds the major assets and a short "pour-over" will that catches anything left out and names guardians for the kids.

## What a Will Does in Utah

A will is your written instructions for what happens after you die. Under [Utah Code § 75-2-502](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/75-2-S502.html), a standard will must be:

1. In writing,
2. Signed by you (or by someone else at your direction and in your presence), and
3. Signed by at least two witnesses who saw you sign or heard you acknowledge the will.

Utah also recognizes **handwritten (holographic) wills** when the signature and the important parts are in your own handwriting. They're legal, but they're also easier for an unhappy relative to challenge.

A will is the place to:

- Name who receives your property
- Name a **personal representative** (Utah's term for an executor) to handle your estate
- **Name a guardian for your minor children.** [Utah Code § 75-5-202](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter5/75-5-S202.html) lets a parent appoint a guardian by will. For parents of young kids, this alone is reason enough to have one.

**What a will doesn't do:** keep your family out of court. A will is essentially a set of instructions *to* the probate court. Your personal representative still has to open a probate case to carry them out.

## What a Revocable Living Trust Does

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create while you're alive. You move your property into the trust, usually naming yourself as trustee, so nothing changes day to day. You can still sell your house, refinance, or change the trust whenever you want.

When you die, the **successor trustee** you chose distributes the property to your family according to your instructions. No court case, no public filing.

A trust also helps if you become **unable to manage your own affairs** because of a stroke, dementia, or an accident. Your successor trustee can step in and pay the bills without anyone asking a court for a conservatorship.

### The catch: a trust only works if it's funded

This is the mistake we see most often. Someone pays for a trust, signs it, puts the binder on a shelf, and never moves anything into it. **A trust can only control property that's titled in its name.** If the house is still in your personal name when you die, it may go through probate anyway.

Funding usually means:

- Signing and recording a new deed that moves your home into the trust (see our [real estate services](/real-estate))
- Retitling bank and brokerage accounts
- Updating beneficiary designations where it makes sense
- Assigning personal property and business interests to the trust

## Will vs. Trust: Side by Side

| | Will | Revocable Living Trust |
|---|---|---|
| **Takes effect** | At death | As soon as it's signed and funded |
| **Probate** | Generally required | Avoided for property in the trust |
| **Privacy** | Becomes part of a public court file | Stays private |
| **If you're incapacitated** | Doesn't help | Successor trustee steps in |
| **Names a guardian for kids** | Yes | No (you still need a will for this) |
| **Upfront cost** | Lower | Higher, because there's more to prepare and fund |
| **Best fit** | Simple estates, young families starting out | Homeowners, blended families, business owners, property in more than one state |

## What Utah Probate Actually Involves

Probate is the court process for proving a will, paying debts, and transferring property. In Utah, a simple, uncontested probate is usually manageable, but it's never instant:

- **Creditors get three months.** Under [Utah Code § 75-3-801](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter3/75-3-S801.html), the personal representative can publish a notice giving creditors three months from the first publication to file claims. The estate generally can't wrap up before that window closes.
- **Court filings are public.** Anyone can look up what's in the case.
- **Costs add up.** Filing fees, publication costs, and attorney fees all come out of the estate.
- **Out-of-state property means more court.** If you own a cabin in Idaho or a rental in Arizona, your family may need a second probate case in that state.

**Small estates can skip it.** If the whole estate is worth **$100,000 or less** after debts and **includes no real estate**, your family may be able to collect property with a simple affidavit 30 days after death, without going to court ([Utah Code § 75-3-1201](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter3/75-3-S1201.html); see the [Utah Courts small estates page](https://www.utcourts.gov/en/self-help/case-categories/probate/small-estates.html)). Once you own a home, though, this option is usually off the table.

## Some Property Skips Probate Either Way

Before deciding you need a trust, take stock of what already passes outside probate:

- **Life insurance and retirement accounts** with a named beneficiary
- **Bank accounts** with a payable-on-death (POD) beneficiary
- **Property held in joint tenancy** with right of survivorship
- **Real estate with a recorded transfer-on-death deed**, which Utah allows under its [Uniform Real Property Transfer on Death Act](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter6/75-6-P4.html)

For some families, a will plus well-planned beneficiary designations does almost everything a trust would. For others, especially with several properties or complicated family situations, keeping track of all those designations becomes its own risk. One outdated beneficiary form can send an account to an ex-spouse.

## What Happens If You Have Neither

If you die without a will or trust, Utah's intestacy laws decide who inherits. That might match what you'd want. It often doesn't.

Take a blended family. Under [Utah Code § 75-2-102](https://le.utah.gov/xcode/Title75/Chapter2/75-2-S102.html), if you have children from a prior relationship, your surviving spouse receives the first **$75,000 plus half** of the remaining probate estate, and the rest goes to your descendants. Your spouse could end up co-owning the family home with your adult children from a first marriage, which is rarely what anyone intended.

And without a will, **no one has been formally named to raise your minor children.** A judge decides.

## So Which Do You Need?

**A will may be enough if:**

- Your estate is modest, and you don't own real estate yet
- Most of your assets already have named beneficiaries
- Your family situation is straightforward
- Your main goal right now is naming a guardian for your kids

**A trust usually makes sense if:**

- You own a home in Utah, or property in another state
- You have a blended family or want to provide for children from different relationships
- You have a child with special needs, or a beneficiary who shouldn't receive a lump sum
- You own a business (see our [business law services](/business))
- Privacy matters to you
- You want a plan in place if you become unable to manage your finances

When in doubt, the decision often comes down to one question: **do you own a home?** For most Utah homeowners, a funded trust saves the family a probate case later.

## Every Plan Needs More Than a Will or Trust

Whichever you choose, a complete estate plan also includes:

- **[Financial power of attorney](/power-of-attorney-advance-directive-utah):** someone you trust can handle your money if you can't.
- **[Advance health care directive](/power-of-attorney-advance-directive-utah):** names who makes medical decisions for you and records your wishes. Utah's law here changed on January 1, 2026, so if yours is more than a couple of years old, it's worth a review.
- **Pour-over will** (with a trust): catches anything left outside the trust.
- **Updated beneficiary designations** that match the rest of your plan.

## A Quick Note on Taxes

Utah does not have its own estate or inheritance tax. The federal estate tax only applies to estates above the federal exemption, which is **$15 million per person in 2026**. For most Utah families, a trust is about avoiding probate and protecting family members, not about taxes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I still need a will if I have a living trust?

Yes. Most trust-based plans include a short pour-over will that moves anything left outside the trust into it. A will is also where parents name a guardian for minor children.

### Does a will avoid probate in Utah?

No. Property that passes under a will generally still goes through probate. Assets with a named beneficiary, joint ownership with survivorship rights, or a recorded transfer-on-death deed pass outside probate either way.

### Is a handwritten will valid in Utah?

It can be. Utah recognizes holographic wills when the signature and the important parts are in your own handwriting. They're easier to challenge, so a properly witnessed will is the safer choice.

### How often should I update my plan?

Review it after any major life change: marriage, divorce, a new child or grandchild, a move, buying property, or starting a business. Otherwise, a quick review every few years keeps beneficiaries and trustees current.
